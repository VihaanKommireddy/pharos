# SAT Grammar & Punctuation Rules (Standard English Conventions)

Distilled from The Critical Reader's "Complete SAT/ACT Grammar and Punctuation Rules" (Erica Meltzer), updated for the digital SAT. These cover the **Boundaries** and **Form, Structure & Sense** skills. Work the rule, don't go by ear (except where noted).

## Core equivalences (the test will never make you choose between equals)

- **Period = Semicolon = Comma+FANBOYS = (and, when joining two sentences) Colon/Dash.** If two of these are both offered and both merely join two independent clauses, neither is the answer — look for what actually distinguishes the options.
- A **colon or dash** can also introduce a list or explanation; a **semicolon cannot**.

## 1. Period / Semicolon — join two independent clauses

- Use between two complete sentences, and before a conjunctive adverb (however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless) that starts a clause: ...city; however, it...
- **Sentence-boundary trap:** read all the way to the period. The start of the second sentence can masquerade as part of the first. (e.g., "...Nobel **Prize. In 1983,** she won..." not "...Prize in 1983, she won...").
- **Comma splice = always wrong.** Two independent clauses joined by only a comma. Tip-off: **comma + pronoun** (it, they, he/she, one, you, I).

## 2. Colon / Dash — introduce a list or explanation

- What comes **before** the colon/dash must be a complete sentence ("Visitors spend time in three main attractions: ..."). Not "a variety of:" and never after "including/such as".
- What comes **after** can be a fragment OR a full sentence.
- On the digital SAT, when two sentences must be separated and no period/semicolon option appears, the **colon** is often the answer (fewer students know it).

## 3. Comma + FANBOYS (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so)

- comma + FANBOYS joins two independent clauses (= period = semicolon).
- **comma + FANBOYS + verb (no new subject) = WRONG.** If the second clause repeats no subject, drop the comma: "London is old **but has** many modern buildings" (not "city, but has"). Shortcut: replace comma+and/but with a period — if the second part can't stand alone, the comma is wrong.

## 4. Dependent clauses

- A dependent clause (because, although, when, until, while, since, before, after, if) that comes **first** is followed by a comma: "Because London is old**, it** has..." No comma when the independent clause comes first.

## 5. Non-essential information (the biggest comma category)

- Set off with **2 commas, 2 dashes, or 2 parentheses** — never mixed (comma…dash is wrong). If you can delete the chunk and the sentence still works, it's non-essential.
- A **single** comma (or dash) where a pair is required is a classic wrong answer.
- **Non-essential single words** (however, though) take **commas only**, not dashes: "It does**, however,** have..."
- **No comma before an open paren**, and almost never before a close paren.

## 6. Names & titles (essential vs. non-essential)

- Only two valid options: **no commas** (essential) or **two commas** (non-essential). A single comma before a mid-sentence name is automatically wrong.
- Test by deleting the name: if the sentence still makes sense, the name is non-essential (use 2 commas: "a University of Washington engineering professor, Vikram Iyer,"). If it breaks, the name is essential (no commas: "engineering professor Vikram Iyer").

## 7. Commas — SHOULD use

- Items in a list (final serial comma optional, not tested).
- Between coordinate adjectives whose order can flip ("active, warm-blooded animals").

## 8. Commas — should NOT use

- **Before/after a preposition** (of, to, by, from, for, about, with, in, on, at).
- **Between subject and verb** (even a long subject): "The oldest bridge in London is..." not "London, is...".
- **Between compound items joined by *and*** (two nouns/verbs/adjectives with one subject). Shortcut: comma+and = period; if that makes a fragment, no comma.
- **Before or after *that*.** And when *that* is omitted ("the snow used to build..."), still no comma.
- Between two adjectives whose order **can't** flip ("stunning coral reefs"), and between an adjective and its noun.

## 9. Apostrophes — plural vs. possessive

- **Nouns:** apostrophe = possessive (singular or plural); no apostrophe = plain plural. Singular: cell's. Regular plural: cells'. Irregular plural: people's.
- Two-noun tip: the pattern is usually noun's noun or nouns' noun (first noun possesses the second).
- **Pronouns are the opposite — no apostrophe = possessive.** its (poss.) vs. it's (= it is); their/theirs vs. they're (= they are) vs. there (place); whose (poss.) vs. who's (= who is). "its'" and "theirs'" don't exist.

## 10. Pronouns & nouns

- **Pronoun–antecedent agreement:** singular thing = it; plural = they/them/their. (The SAT does **not** use singular *they*.) Match the actual referent, which may be in a previous sentence.
- Noun agreement: "are **examples**," not "are **an example**."
- **who/whom = people; which = things; whose = both.**
- **whom before a verb = wrong** (use who); **whom after a preposition = right** (to whom).

## 11. Subject–verb agreement

- Singular 3rd-person verb ends in -s (it works); plural does not (they work). to be: is/was (sg.), are/were (pl.).
- Find the **true subject** at the start of the clause; ignore intervening **prepositional phrases** ("Illegal logging in Mexican forests **has**...") and **non-essential clauses** ("The cochineal, a small insect..., **was**...").
- **Compound subject (X and Y) = plural.**
- **Each, every, either, neither (as subject) = singular.** Collective nouns (group, team, government, country) = singular. There is + singular / There are + plural.
- Watch **inverted order** (verb before subject): "In the nucleus sit elementary particles."

## 12. Verb tense

- **Consistency:** match surrounding verbs; don't shift tense without reason.
- Correct answers are almost always **simple past or present**; ornate tenses (would have done, will have gone) are nearly always wrong.
- **Present perfect (has/have + verb)** for an action continuing into the present; tip-offs **for / since**.
- **Simple past** for a completed past action with a fixed date ("published Silent Spring in 1962," not "has published").
- **Past perfect (had + verb)** required with **"by the time"**.
- **Tense questions are often disguised subject-verb-agreement questions** — different tenses offered, but only one matches the subject's number. Check number first.
- **to vs. -ing** (infinitive vs. gerund) and verb finiteness: if the clause already has a main verb, the blank is usually a **participle**, not a second finite verb.

## 13. Parallel structure & word pairs

- Lists (3+) and paired items (joined by and/but) must share form: noun/noun/noun, -ing/-ing/-ing, verb/verb/verb, to+verb / (to)+verb.
- **Word pairs must stay matched:** either…or, neither…nor, not only…but (also), as…as, more/less…than — and the structures on each side must be parallel.

## 14. Modifiers

- **Dangling modifier:** an opening descriptive phrase must be immediately followed by the noun it describes. "Born in Mexico City in 1907, **Frida Kahlo** was best known..." (not "...self-portraits were...").
- Same logic mid-sentence: a trailing participial phrase ("enacted in 1993," "edited by X") attaches to the noun it logically modifies — punctuate so it sits next to that noun.

## 15. Faulty comparisons

- Compare like to like: people↔people, things↔things. Use **that of** (sing.) / **those of** (pl.), or a possessive, to compare the right things: "the work of Marshall is as well-known as **that of** Wright."
- **than** (comparison) vs. **then** (time).
- **number/many/fewer** + countable plural; **amount/much/less** + uncountable singular.

## 16. Question marks

- Only for **direct** questions. An indirect/embedded question takes a **period**: "...designed to test how much children understand about the world." (period, normal word order) — a recurring digital-SAT item.

## 17. Concision (digital SAT "Form, Structure & Sense")

- When several choices are grammatical and say the same thing, the **shortest, non-redundant** one wins. Cut wordiness and repetition; avoid needless passive voice.

## 18. Diction / idioms

- **could/should/would/might HAVE**, never "of."
- Preposition + verb idioms have no rule — judge by ear ("effective **at** keeping," not "with keeping").
- Register: pick the choice that's neither too casual ("a whole bunch") nor too ornate ("a sublime plethora") — "a remarkable variety."
