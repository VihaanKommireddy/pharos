Pharos: Zero-Install Pool-Safety Detection on a Phone You Already Own

Stillness & submersion alerts, measured across real pools and published in the open
Vihaan Kommireddy, Aryan Mahalingham, Dhruva Valluru
Independent student research · Cary, North Carolina · Science-fair & Congressional App Challenge 2026
Try the live demo

Overview

Every camera that watches a pool starts with a $1,269–$1,299 hardware install, and not one vendor publishes how often it fails. Pharos runs on the phone in your pocket — no install — and raises an alarm when a swimmer goes still too long or slips under and does not resurface. It is the first pool detector measured across many real pools, in the open.

Key Contributions

1

Zero-install detection

Runs in a browser on a phone families already own. No hardware, no mount, no calibration rig — the differentiator ten funded competitors cannot copy without cannibalizing their own product.

2

Measurable triggers, not “distress”

Alarms on stillness past a threshold and a track that submerges and never returns — signals a camera can measure and a human can verify, never a subjective classifier.

3

First cross-pool benchmark

An independent detection accuracy measured across multiple real pools and published — the number every vendor omits and no one else releases.

4

An honest negative result

We show that off-the-shelf AI is blind to people in water — then fine-tune a detector to fix it, and publish the before/after.

Problem & Motivation

Drowning is fast, silent, and the leading cause of death for U.S. children ages 1–4 (CDC); in a 1,537-case CPSC analysis, 80% of drowned toddlers were alone. Ten companies in five countries sell AI pool cameras, but each is a fixed $1,299 install tied to one pool, and every “99%” claim is self-report with no false-alarm rate, miss rate, or time-to-alert. The people most exposed to drowning are the least able to buy the hardware — and no one can compare systems that publish nothing.

Method

1 · Overhead capture
Phone browser + above-water camera; a 10-second finger-trace defines the water — no install.
2 · Person detection
MoveNet MultiPose (TensorFlow.js) — up to 6 people, 17 keypoints each, on-device.
3 · Tracking
Centroid + IoU tracker holds a stable ID per swimmer, with a grace window for dropouts.
4 · Motion signature
Limb motion relative to each body’s own centroid, torso-normalized — a drifting still body still reads as still.
5 · Triggers
Stillness past N seconds (adaptive to camera noise) OR a track lost in the pool and not reacquired.
6 · Alert
On-device siren + full-screen takeover + phone push + one-tap 911. Every frame logged to CSV.

Key Features

Results

1
The pipeline works. 22 unit tests pass; verified end-to-end in a browser on demo and live video at ~9–26 fps. The engineering is not the bottleneck.
2
Detector-blindness (key finding). Across three real pools, stock MoveNet detected 0% of in-water swimmers — a second model (COCO-SSD) agreed — while both found dry bodies on floats instantly. Night pool: 0 / 762 frames.
3
Real rescue footage. On fixed-camera pool CCTV of an actual child rescue, the submersion trigger fired — “check the water.”

Impact & Future Work

Now: fine-tune a YOLO swimmer detector on licensed pool imagery and publish In-Water Swimmer Recall (stock vs. fine-tuned, per pool). Next: hand-label the team’s own overhead pool footage to match a real mount, then measure across a YMCA, a hotel, and a neighborhood pool — the cross-pool number nobody else can produce.

Pharos detects stillness and submersion — not “drowning,” which no camera can truly know. It assists a lifeguard’s or parent’s supervision; it never replaces it.
